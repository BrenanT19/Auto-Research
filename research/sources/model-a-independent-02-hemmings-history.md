It’s the car that helped build one of America’s great automotive institutions.

Ford Motor Company? Well, yes, but actually, we were thinking, Hemmings Motor News.

Back in the days when Hemmings was divided into Ford and non-Ford ads, Model A cars for sale, parts for sale and related services made up a significant portion of our business, second only, of course, to the Model T.

The A still occupies a substantial place among our pages as well as in the hearts of almost every American automobile enthusiast. If you don’t own a Model A, you probably know someone who does, and you’ve undoubtedly gazed at rows of Model As at car shows in all of their many forms–stock as well as modified.

What’s remarkable about the A is its staying power as a collector car, due to its versatility and its availability. Though the A was produced for just four model years, it was offered in dozens of body styles, from two-doors, four-doors, roadsters, haulers and almost every combination in between. Ford built 2 million Model As in less than two model years, and total worldwide production was more than 5 million. Today, it’s estimated that 500,000 Model As are still out there, so it’s probably safe to say that there’s one to suit any taste and practically any budget.

As sequels go, the Model A could rival The Godfather II for outdistancing a legendary predecessor. Ford cranked out 15 million Model Ts over a production run lasting nearly two decades, thus establishing Dearborn as the car builder for the masses. But the Model A was the engineering and styling leap forward that Ford badly needed to remain competitive in the face of tough competition from the likes of Chevrolet and upstart Plymouth.

Among the many advancements that Ford rolled out in the A:

•A 200.5-cu.in. L-head four-cylinder engine that boasted 40 hp at 2,200 RPM, almost double the horsepower of the Model T’s powerplant;

•The T’s old pedal-operated planetary transmission was gone, replaced by a more modern three-speed selective sliding-gear transmission with a conventional clutch and floor shift. The T’s hand throttle was out too, and the Model A’s accelerator pedal was in;

•The T’s single band brake was replaced with four-wheel mechanical drum brakes in the A;

•To smooth out the bumps, hydraulic shock absorbers were standard equipment. Other Ford production firsts that debuted on the A included a safety glass windshield, Bendix self starter, grease fittings on the chassis, battery and coil ignition, and a water pump to circulate the coolant.

From the outside, the changes were obvious. The Model A looked sleek, smooth and low alongside the Model T, borrowing sculpted front fenders and a graceful radiator shell almost exactly from Lincoln. Six passenger-car body types were advertised initially–Roadster, Coupe, Phaeton, Sport Coupe, Tudor Sedan and Fordor–plus a pickup. The Fordor became available later in 1928, and the line was expanded further throughout 1928 and 1929. A major facelift came about in 1930, and further updates were made in 1931. By the end of the A’s run, the number of body variations had ballooned to upwards of 50. Color was a new hallmark of the Model A as well, with more than three dozen shades applied from the factory over the course of the A’s tenure.

Despite pressure from inside his own company spearheaded by his son, Edsel, Henry Ford didn’t decide to replace the T with a more modern car until the summer of 1926. Prior to that, Ford had pinned his hopes on a successor to the Model T that would be powered by an unusual eight-cylinder X-configuration engine, with four banks of twin cylinders arranged opposite each other in the shape of an X–one set at two o’clock, another at four o’clock, another at eight o’clock and the fourth at 10 o’clock. Edsel and engineers within Ford, meanwhile, were favoring an inline-six.

With the competition gnawing away at the Model T, Ford grudgingly decided to go with a more powerful four-cylinder in a car called the Model A. In a move that would be unthinkable today, production of the Model T ended in May 1927, and seven months passed before the first Model A was sold. Thousands of assembly line workers were laid off as the River Rouge plant tooled up for Model A production, in addition to 34 plants in North America and more than a dozen plants overseas.

Ford dealers passed the time selling the Model Ts they had left in inventory, selling parts and performing service work, or selling used cars. Dozens of dealers quit Ford, unable to survive the loss of income. Finally, after months of secrecy, the Model A was introduced on December 2, 1927. According to Ford News, 10.5 million people turned out nationwide for a glimpse at the new Ford. In the first two weeks, eager motorists ordered 400,000 Model As, as Ford struggled to build 100 cars per day. By the end of 1928, more than 6,000 new Model As were being built per day.

Due to the changeover from Model T to Model A production, Chevrolet built and sold more cars than Ford in 1927 and 1928. In 1929, Chevrolet was relegated to second place, but its new overhead-valve, 194-cu.in. six-cylinder engine was making waves. Meanwhile Chrysler had made waves of its own with the Plymouth, introduced in 1928. With hydraulic brakes and elegant styling in a competitively priced, though slightly more expensive package, it too would keep Ford from resting on its laurels too long.

The Big Three were off and running, led by the Model A, creating loyal fans who would form separate camps–Ford and non-Ford–cherishing, restoring and collecting these cars for years to come.

Horsepower: 40

Torque: 128-lb.ft. at 1,000 RPM

Transmission: Three-speed selective

Drive Axle: 3/4 floating with 8.4-inch-diameter ring gear
